### 8.93 The cheating coin one level up: fairness levels, simple and deep cheats, and where cheats come from (2026-10-13 12:05 BST)

*The owner (2026-10-13), after 8.92: "I could also extract it to the higher domain over the fundamental problem itself -

very toss of the entire problem is cheating." Single-party (AGENTS.md item 16). Probe:

(density rules out odd periods and lopsided blocks); 'de Bruijn' -> used for counting (8.77, 8.86, G127), no fairness
ladder; 'injective' with 'finite config' -> G121; 'column reading 0 1' -> L433; 'Kolmogorov', 'logical depth' -> no hit.*

**1. Every finite level of fairness is passed by a periodic cheat.** Call a periodic word *k-fair* when every block of
k bits occurs equally often around its cycle. Then (elementary): a k-fair word's least period is a multiple of 2^k,
because each of the 2^k blocks occurs p/2^k times; and at period exactly 2^k the k-fair words are the de Bruijn
cycles, which hold every k-block once. 8.34's remark is the case k = 1: density 1/2 rules out every odd period and
every lopsided block, and 01, the de Bruijn cycle of order 1, passes. Fairness in pairs rules out every period not
divisible by 4 and is passed by 0011; fairness in triples is passed by 00010111; and so on up. Computed over every
primitive word to length 16: k-fair words occur only at multiples of 2^k, at every such multiple, and number exactly 2,
4, 16 and 256 at period 2^k for k = 1 .. 4 (the de Bruijn count 2^(2^(k-1)) with rotations). So fairness at any finite
level cannot exclude every periodic cheat; only fairness at every level, normality, does, and normality is far beyond
Problem 2. Period 2 is the cheat at the bottom of the ladder, the first period the average cannot see.

**2. The whole toss is a cheat, in a precise sense.** The single seed's centre column is computable: a short program
(run Rule 30 for n ticks) prints its first n bits, so their Kolmogorov complexity is of order log n, the same order as
0101's. In the algorithmic sense the column is not random at all, which is the owner's point: it is a cheating coin
that passes the statistical tests (density 0.49947 over 2^17 ticks and a coin's block entropy, 8.34; the check below).
So no argument of the form "the column is random, so it cannot be periodic" can work, because by description length
the fair-looking column and the periodic cheat are equally simple. What tells them apart is cost: the n-th bit of
0101 costs order log n to compute, and Problem 3 asks whether the n-th bit of Rule 30's column costs order n. In this
language the three prizes ask one question three ways [J]: is the cheat shallow (Problem 1), is it balanced (Problem 2),
is it deep (Problem 3)? A deep cheat cannot be shallow, which is 8.34's "Problem 3 implies Problem 1".

*The block's unexpected check: the pair side at depth.* The column shows all 2^k blocks of k bits by ticks within a
factor 0.68 .. 1.76 of the mean over 16 coin sequences, for every k = 1 .. 14 (all 16,384 blocks of 14 bits by tick
143,603, against a coin mean of 164,283). The controls fail as they must: 0101... never shows 00, and Rule 90's
single-seed centre column never shows 01.

**3. Where cheats come from: a finite state space, or merging.**
- *Rings.* On a ring every column is eventually periodic, because the ring has finitely many states. The 0101 cheat
  appears only on rings of 7, 14 and 21 cells up to 21 (L433, replicated), and it is one rigid object: every cheating
  cycle is the seven-cell cheat repeated, with temporal period 4, and exactly 4 states alternate in column 0 from tick
  0 at every size (4 of 128, of 16,384 and of 2,097,152), as Collatz's plateau is exactly one start per phase (8.92).
- *Basins.* The states that eventually fall into the cheat number 49 of 128 at seven cells, 1,085 of 16,384 (6.6
  percent) at fourteen, and 49 of 2,097,152 at twenty-one. The blind prediction that the basin would stay under 5
  percent at 14 and 21 cells was refuted at 14. The reason, found after the run: 1,036 of the 1,085 are non-repeating
  states whose orbit becomes 7-periodic after 1 to 41 ticks. That needs two states, x and its shift by 7, to share an
  image; the fourteen-cell ring has 813 images with two or more preimages. A basin is built by merging.
- *Collatz* merges (T(1) = T(4) = 2) and shrinks its numbers on average, so its cheat is the end of every orbit tested.
- *Rule 30 on the line* does neither. It is injective on finite configurations (G121), and both edges of a finite
  pattern advance one cell every tick (the cells just outside see 001 and 100, which make black, and those beyond see
  000), so no two finite orbits ever merge and none revisits a state. A cheater on the line, if one exists, has no
  whole-state basin: no other orbit flows into its orbit. That does not make cheaters few. GPT's four-state control
  (GC1165: two tracks, Rule 150 coupled, injective on finite rows, growing one cell a tick at each end) has
  infinitely many disjoint finite orbits that all share an exactly period-2 centre column. A shared column tail is
  not a whole-state basin. What stays true is the reach: only a statement about every single seed can exclude a
  cheater, a count that falls below one (8.92's price) or a theorem that uses the edge, as Jen's and Condrey's do.
- *Mahler's problem* sits on Rule 30's side: its map expands, and its cheats (Z-numbers) are proved sparse, at most
  x^gamma below x with gamma < 1 (Flatto, Lagarias and Pollington; PRIOR-ART.md), but not proved absent.
- [J], an interpretation only, and REFUTED as a general inference by GC1165. In the three problems the direction of
  each conjecture matches the dynamics: where states merge and shrink (Collatz) the conjecture is that every seed
  cheats, and where they never merge and the state grows (Rule 30, Mahler's expanding map) it is that none does. GPT's
  control shows injectivity and growth together do not prevent cheating, so this is a pattern in these three problems,
  not a premise any argument may carry.

**2. Numbers that cannot be written down.** The owner: "if something can't be computed it follows something can't be

- *The counting runs the other way.* Turing's computable numbers can be listed by their machines' description numbers,

  reals cannot be written down at all.
- *The prizes' objects can all be written down.* Rule 30 is one rule and one cell, Collatz starts from an integer, and

  points from the rest, and they say nothing about a measure-zero set such as the integers. That is §8.47's gap between

    counterexample would be a finite proof that it is false.
  - Rule 30's period 2, Collatz and Mahler are $\Pi_2$ (§8.46; PRIOR-ART.md) and have no such safety net.
  - Machines whose behaviour ZFC cannot settle exist. Yedidia and Aaronson (2016) built one of 7,918 states, and later

    provability gives out between those is unknown, and so is the side Collatz-type questions fall on.
  - Nothing is evidence that these conjectures are independent of PA, still less of ZFC (GC1221). The best-known
    natural statements independent of PA (Paris–Harrington, Goodstein) are provable in ZFC.

**3. Time, and the Bézier shortcut.** The owner: "the application of the rule requires a state machine that is able to

- *De Casteljau is a linear cellular automaton.* Each row is $(1-t)\,\text{left} + t\,\text{right}$ of the row above,

```math
x_t \;=\; L^t x_0 \;\oplus\; \bigoplus_{s=0}^{t-1} L^{\,t-1-s}\, N(x_s).
```

- *What the form says.* The first term is the jump. The sum is an echo from every earlier time the AND fired, and
  finding those times needs the whole history.
  - PROOF-SKETCH: a standard variation-of-constants identity by a one-line induction, not filed in PROOFS.md as new.

- *Where it meets the shader.* This is where BIDIRECTIONAL-AS-MATHEMATICS.md §12.1 found linear algebra stopping. The

- *A time shortcut is not enough.* Mahler and Erdős have one: $(3/2)^n$ and $2^n$ are reached directly ($2^n$ in

### 8.103 Compounded shortcuts, and the kick's echoes as interference (2026-10-14 16:45 BST, Cloud)

*Two prompts from the owner after 8.102. First, on S6: "Echoes conspiring sounds like interference." Second: "What

140-step class-12 exclusion), 8.4's L1 (98.1% of slips at wheel phases 32 and 52), Terras (COLLATZ-PRIZE.md section
1) and Saye's free identity (CONSTELLATION.md G.1).

**1. Compounded jumps, answered from the record.** An exact jump composes exactly: jumping a steps and then b steps
is the jump of a + b, so compounding never adds error. What differs between the problems is how fast the jumps can
go and what each one needs. There are three regimes.
- *Speeding up, rooted, for ever: linear rules.* Over GF(2), squaring is free:
  $(1 + z + z^{-1})^2 = 1 + z^2 + z^{-2}$. So the jump to time $2^k$ is three cells, and the jump to any $t$ follows
  the binary digits of $t$ in $\log_2 t$ jumps. This is GC487's logarithmic indexing, and it works exactly when the
  sequence's 2-kernel is finite, that is, when the sequence is automatic. The jumps double each time and never drift
  from the pattern.
- *Steady speed, rooted, paying as they go: the free parts of Collatz, Mahler and Erdős.*
  - The jumps are exact, but each one consumes information. Terras: k Collatz steps need n mod 2^k, one bit of the
    start per step. Saye: the last k ternary digits of 2^n need n mod 2 x 3^(k-1).
  - The jumps go on for ever, but only as fast as the start can be read. For Erdős they slow down relative to the
    object: of the roughly 0.63 n ternary digits of 2^n, the free jump reaches only the last log_3 n or so, and the rest
    is the constrained part.
- *No speed-up: Rule 30's columns.*
  - Column 1's 2-kernel grows like a random sequence's (T6: 492 of 512 at depth 9). The table that doubling jumps would
    need grows as fast as the data, so a jump costs as much as the walk.
  - The approximate jumps of 8.100 (the Rule 150 jump, echoes dropped) also compose exactly: compounding them is one
    long Rule 150 jump. So they stay rooted, but in the wrong pattern, Rule 150's Sierpiński triangles. These agree
    with Rule 30 only as well as a coin (36 of 65 in 8.100; R(32, B) about 0.5 in 8.102).
  - Decoherence is immediate and total, and compounding neither adds to it nor removes it.
- *"We can't prove whether the coins before us were fair."* An exact jump lands exactly and needs no fairness. An
  approximate jump lands on a coin, and that coin's fairness is Wolfram's Problem 2, which is open.

**2. The kick's echoes as interference** (`tests/probes/lexicon/rule30_cloud_echo_interference.py`; predictions I1 to

parity is an interference amplitude: plus or minus 1 is fully coherent, 0 is decohered. For each lag m before a kick,

### 8.109 Door (c): Mahler's admissible set meets its doubles and triples only at 0 (2026-10-14 20:02 BST, Cloud)

*Door (c) of CONSTELLATION.md section H (INSTINCT-CLOUD.md S2), at the owner's word: "c please". The hunch was that
Mahler's map acts on the real and 2-adic sides at once, so the pair of places might supply the second multiplier that
Furstenberg's x2 x3 theorem needs. Predictions were pushed before the run (2306731). Single-party (Cloud). The
theorem below is a hand proof, so PROOF-SKETCH, with every step checked by exact computation to depth 28.*

Record searched: S_strict, times 3, 2/3, invariant under, admissible, Renyi, solenoid -> MAHLER.md section 1 (G50, G51,
GC1221, GC1222, GC1224, the census A(T)), 8.100, 8.101; no hit for a multiplicative symmetry of the admissible set.

**Setting** (MAHLER.md section 1). T(x) = (3x + (x mod 2))/2 on the 2-adic integers Z_2. The code b(x) is the sequence
of parities of T^j(x), and u_j = sum_k b_(j+k) 2^k / 3^(k+1).
- S_strict is the set of x with every u_j < 1/2, and K is its closure: the x whose every finite prefix of b(x) is
  admissible (GC1222).
- For every x, x = -S_2(b(x)): the code's series summed 2-adically. So for a positive integer n, n is the 2-adic point
  itself, and a Z-number with integer part n exists exactly when n lies in S_strict (G50).
- Two facts carry everything:
  - (F1) the length-T prefix of b(x) depends only on x mod 2^T, bijectively;
  - (F2) admissible prefixes are closed under taking prefixes, p is admissible exactly when 0p is, a 1 is always
    followed by a 0, and 0^k 1 is admissible for every k.

**Lemma 1** (PROOF-SKETCH). For every y in Z_2, 2y lies in K exactly when 3y does, and likewise for S_strict.
- 2y is even and T(2y) = 3y, so b(2y) = 0 b(3y), and (F2) gives the claim for K.
- For S_strict, u_0(2y) = (2/3) u_0(3y) < 1/3 whenever 3y qualifies.
- Taking y = z/3 shows K is invariant under z -> 2z/3, the inverse branch of T on the even numbers. That is the
  Mahler direction itself.

**Theorem** (PROOF-SKETCH). If x and 3x both lie in K, then x = 0. Hence K meets 3K, K/3, 2K and K/2 only in 0, and so
does S_strict.

*Proof.* Let A_T be the residues mod 2^T whose length-T prefix is admissible, and let
s(T) = {r in A_T : 3r mod 2^T in A_T}. The claim is that s(T) = {0, 2^(T-1)} for every T >= 1.
- *Base.* At T = 1 both prefixes are admissible and 3 = 1 mod 2, so s(1) = {0, 1}.
- *Step: the candidates.* Reduction mod 2^T maps s(T+1) into s(T), by (F1) and (F2). So s(T+1) lies among the lifts
  0, 2^T, 2^(T-1) and 3 * 2^(T-1).
- *How a code starts.* A residue 2^k m with m odd and k <= T has code 0^k 1 c, where c is the parity of
  (3^(k+1) m + 1)/2. That parity depends only on m mod 4, and by (F2) the prefix is admissible exactly when c = 0.
- *The four lifts.*
  - 0 and 2^T stay. Their codes are all zeros, or 0^T 1, for the residue and its triple alike.
  - For r = 2^(T-1), c = 0 exactly when 3^T = 3 mod 4, that is, when T is odd. For its triple, with m = 3 mod 4,
    c = 0 exactly when T is even. The two conditions never hold together, so r drops out.
  - For r = 3 * 2^(T-1), admissibility needs T even, and its triple, which lies in the class of 2^(T-1), needs T odd.
    So it drops out too.
- *So* s(T+1) = {0, 2^T}.

If x and 3x lie in K, then x mod 2^T lies in s(T) for every T, so 2^(T-1) divides x for every T, and x = 0. The other
three intersections follow from Lemma 1: if 2x and x lie in K, then 3x does too. QED.

**Computation** (`tests/probes/mahler/mahler_cloud_two_multipliers.py`).
- *Controls.*

  - C1: |A_T| = 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 27, 40, 60, 90, exactly MAHLER.md section 1's A(T).
  - C2: Lemma 1's finite form holds at every depth to 24.

  T = 2 .. 28.
- *Predictions.*

  - P2 was refuted. f3 falls by exactly 2/3 a step (0.666 to 0.668), not by 0.7 to 0.95, because exactly two residues

    falling about 3/4 a step (0.0053 at T = 24, fifty times f3).

**In Mahler's terms.**
- If n is the integer part of a Z-number, then 2n and 3n are not, and neither are n/2 and n/3 when they are integers.
- 2n/3 is the integer part of a Z-number whenever 3 divides n. On the real side, (2/3)xi is then a Z-number: its first

The backward chain is probably classical. The disjointness was not checked against the literature.

### 8.115 First principles: what Wolfram set out to do, why Rule 30 is sticky, and the wall's name in complexity theory (2026-10-15 12:29 BST, Cloud)

*The owner's step-back: "consider first principles going right back to the root of the problem as Wolfram invented
it: 1. Wolfram set out to look systematically at the simplest possible programs of this kind. 2. The observation that
a very simple rule can produce behaviour which looks random ... What are we missing here in the bigger picture."
Conversation pinned at the owner's word; a reading, not a result. Single-party (Cloud). The owner's verdict: "a brand
new lens and reframing ... It therefore takes priority"; a small investigation with GPT first (CL392), then a thesis.*

Record searched: natural proof, Razborov, Agafonov, normal number, Kolakoski, cosmological, Goodstein, flocking,
logistic map, Langton, Champernowne, Kolmogorov, Antihydra -> no hit for natural proofs, Agafonov, Kolakoski,
flocking or the logistic map; Razborov once (8.113's critic, circuit lower bounds as a missing lens); Goodstein and
Champernowne in 8.23 and 8.26 (columns as numbers); Antihydra in 26 places (the Collatz-like 6-state machine);
Langton in CONSTELLATION.md G.

**Why Wolfram looked, and what drew him.** A physicist's embarrassment: the second law, turbulence and the forms of
nature look as if they need randomness or complicated laws, and the known laws are neither. He stopped asking which
rule produces nature and asked what rules in general produce, by enumeration. The 256 elementary automata were the
smallest cage he could enumerate completely, and Rule 30 was the specimen that justified the expedition: the first
thing in the cage that looked like the second law. His later name for the lesson is computational irreducibility.

**Why it is sticky.** The rule fits in a head whole (eight cases), so the mind expects to see through it, and the
output refuses: the primes' itch. The sharper reason is self-referential. His thesis is "no shortcut"; a proof is a
shortcut, a finite object settling infinitely many cases. So the thesis predicts his own prize problems are hard, for
the reason he finds them interesting.

**Did he succeed at question 1?** At his scale, completely. But "simplest possible" is relative to a description
language, and the invariance theorem makes every choice right up to a constant. The honest test is the next scale,
done rigorously by others: BB(5) was proved in 2024, machine-checked, an exhaustive understanding of every 5-state
Turing machine, and BB(6) is blocked by machines whose halting is a Collatz-like question (Antihydra, already in the
record). The systematic sweep, carried out by a community forty years on, succeeds exactly up to this project's wall
and stops there. Evidence that the wall is generic to simple programs, not a quirk of Rule 30.

**"This kind", and what sits beside it.** Each adjective in "discrete, deterministic, local, synchronous,
homogeneous, one-dimensional, two-state" is a choice, and each relaxation is a known field.
- Continuous state: deterministic chaos (the logistic map, Lorenz, the double pendulum), where randomness is proved
  in the measure sense (almost every seed) and open for any specific seed. Rule 30 is the discrete cousin with the
  fewest handles: no continuous coordinate to carry a measure through a conjugacy.
- Substitution systems: automatic sequences (Thue-Morse; Rule 90's column), completely understood by Cobham and
  Christol. Exactly the simple rules with a time shortcut (the nth term from the digits of n): 8.100's shortcut theme
  formalised. Problem 3 conjectures Rule 30 has none.
- Agents and asynchrony: flocking (Reynolds, Vicsek), Life, traffic, Langton's ant.
- Non-local rules: networks and hypergraph rewriting, where Wolfram went in 2020, with the observer as the central
  notion. He has, in effect, conceded that "looks random" is a statement about the observer.

**Is observation 2 valid?** In the strongest sense it is false: the column is computable, so its Kolmogorov
complexity is about log n, and it is random to no algorithmic test. It is random to a bounded observer, and the three
prize problems are a ladder of observers:
- Problem 1 (not periodic): it beats the observer that checks for repetition.
- Problem 2 (density 1/2): it beats the simplest finite-state gambler. Beating every finite-state gambler is
  normality (Schnorr and Stimm; Agafonov's theorem is the finite-state selection statement: corrected after GC1265),
  so problem 2, which is simple normality of order 1, is the first rung of "the column is a normal number". The rungs
  are different axes, as GPT notes: 0101... is balanced and periodic, the squares' indicator is aperiodic with density
  0, and normality implies neither hard indexing (Champernowne's word is indexed in polylog time, GC542) nor problem
  3.
- Problem 3 (no shortcut): it beats every fast observer; a linear-time lower bound for one specific function.
The same ladder as the digits of sqrt 2: computable, believed normal, proved normal for nothing but constructed
numbers (Champernowne). Rule 30's column is sqrt 2 with a worse rule.

**Flocking, the opposite direction.** In the Vicsek model noise goes in and order comes out, with a proved phase
transition; Rule 30 puts order in and randomness comes out. "Perfectly reproducible" cuts both ways: Rule 30 is also
reproducible; the flock is not random to one who knows the rule, and still cannot be predicted without running it.
That is the honest form of observation 2: not random, but incompressible to this observer.

**Other simple rules that look random, and what was proved.** Langton's ant: unboundedness proved (parity), the
highway for every finite start open. Kolakoski: density 1/2 open (problem 2's twin). Conway's look-and-say: proved
(the cosmological theorem) by finite analysis of how strings split into 92 independent atoms. Goodstein: proved by
transfinite induction, unprovable in Peano arithmetic (Kirby and Paris): the wall was the proof system. The primes:
every proved statement is about density, averages, almost all. Everything proved is measure-theoretic, algebraic (a
shortcut), or a decomposition into independent finite pieces. The record found Rule 30 has no shortcut (measured)
and no decomposition (kicks have no memory, 8.43; the echo picture is exact but not local, 8.102).

**What we are missing: the wall has a name.** Razborov and Rudich's natural proofs theorem (1994) says, roughly,
that any proof technique which works by showing a specific function "looks random" to efficient tests (a property
that is large, holding for most functions, and constructive, decidable efficiently) cannot prove lower bounds if
strong pseudorandom generators exist. Wolfram built a rule he conjectures to be a good pseudorandom generator, and
asked us to prove it looks random. Read through Razborov and Rudich, his conjecture is the conjecture that no
natural proof of it exists. The "ineffable wall across similar problems" the owner felt has, for circuits, been a
theorem for thirty years. Whether it bears formally on any of the three was CL392's question for GPT. *Answered
(GC1265, 12:55 BST; my kill test tips to "analogy only"):* the primary reads establish no natural-proofs barrier
for any prize. Problem 3 as stated is a uniform time bound in the index n (GC548 and CL035 already separated the
display's "no O(n)" from the prose's "no shortcut"); a circuit family f_m(z) = c(value(z)) can be named, but a time
bound does not by itself place f outside P/poly, and the barrier needs a constructive, large, useful property
against a stated class with a matching cryptographic assumption. Problems 1 and 2 supply no circuit-class exclusion.
And calling the record's proofs "non-natural" is unsupported until a property and class are named; Razborov and
Rudich themselves naturalise Smolensky's algebraic argument. So the honest form of the lesson is weaker and still
useful: the proofs that exist for specific objects (Condrey, Proposition 19, the sign rule, the kick cone, the
cosmological theorem) each use a structure of the object, never a randomness property; 8.114's coin-shape test is a
randomness property, and could only ever fail to find anything. The barrier is an analogy that poses questions, not
a theorem that prohibits statistical approaches.

**Verdicts.** Reasoned (revised after GC1265): a reframing, not a result; three names for the wall (Borel's
almost-all, Schnorr-Stimm's finite-state gamblers, Razborov-Rudich's natural proofs, the last as analogy) and a
selection rule for methods. GPT's unexpected check: a normal initial row gives a normal column (G4.4 and G128 realise
any one-sided trace), so the single-seed and finite-support cases are the open ones. Gut: the strongest pull of
the week, and the same unease as always about a lens that explains why nothing works.

### 8.116 Thesis: the prize is a question about observers, and the record is a catalogue of which observers the column defeats (2026-10-15 12:57 BST, Cloud)

*The owner, after 8.115: "a small investigation with GPT, then after GPT reports you use that finding to expand out a

reading of the record and the literature, with questions at the end that have kill tests. Nothing in it changes a
status. Single-party (Cloud); GPT reads it as a thesis.*

**1. The two statements, restated so that they can be true.** Wolfram's first statement was a programme, and it
succeeded at its own scale and, carried on by others, exactly up to this project's wall: BB(5) is settled and BB(6)
is held by Collatz-like machines. His second statement, "a simple rule produces behaviour that looks random", is
false in the strongest sense (the column is computable, so no algorithmic test is fooled for ever) and true in the
only sense that can be made exact: *the column defeats every observer in a named class*. A prize problem is a
claim of that form, and the record's measurements are a catalogue of which observers the column has defeated so far.

**2. The ladder, with the right names** (GC1265; corrected after GC1267). The three prize problems are not three
rungs of one ladder, and not three independent axes either: the record has a partial implication diagram.
Problem 3 implies problem 1 (8.34: an eventually periodic column is indexed in O(log n) time, so a no-sublinear-
indexing theorem excludes eventual periodicity; likewise an explicit finite automaton gives O(log n) indexing,
GC487). Problems 1 and 2 separate in both directions, and normality does not imply a hard index (GC542). The
examples:
- *Periodicity* (problem 1): the observer that checks for repetition. 0101... is balanced and periodic.
- *Simple normality of order 1* (problem 2): the weakest finite-state gambler. The indicator of the squares is
  aperiodic and has density 0. The full finite-state class is normality (Schnorr and Stimm), and the record's h_10
  and 8.114's run shapes are finite-state observers of intermediate strength, all defeated at 2^22.
- *Indexing cost* (problem 3): the time to produce the nth bit from n, a uniform bound in the index (GC548, CL035).
  Normality does not imply a hard index: Champernowne's word is normal and indexed in polylog time (GC542). So
  problem 3 is the strongest of the three in one direction only (it implies 1, not 2), and the barrier theorem of
  complexity theory does not reach it as stated (GC1265: a circuit family can be named, but no property, class and
  assumption have been).
The honest summary of forty years of measurement, ours included, is: every observer anyone has built loses to the
column, and no observer has been proved to lose.

**3. What is proved, for which objects, on each axis.** The literature's wins sort cleanly, and the sorting is the
thesis.

  indexing polylog. The shortcut makes the first axis easy and the second impossible.
- *Constructed objects.* Champernowne, Copeland-Erdős, Stoneham numbers: normality proved by construction, index
  cheap. The object was built to defeat the finite-state class.
- *Almost all objects.* Borel: almost every real is normal; almost every seed gives a coin column (8.114; GPT's
  check that a normal row transports to a normal column through G4.4 and G128). The measure proves it for the
  class and names no member.
- *The one simple object* (sqrt 2, pi, Rule 30's column from one cell, the primes' parity, Kolakoski): every axis
  open beyond what a shortcut or a finite computation gives. Within the search GPT made, no finite-seed cellular
  automaton column is proved normal: NOT FOUND, not a global claim. Simple normality of order 1 is elementary for a
  finite seed: rule 50, F(l, c, r) = (1 - c)(l OR r), from a single cell has support {|j| <= t, j = t mod 2}, so its
  centre column is 1010... (GC1267's control, checked here: every old black cell turns white, every opposite-parity

So the wall of 8.45 and 8.101 (no emptiness from a constrained side of positive entropy; theorems at bounded
complexity only) is one face of a pattern the examples obey. *Stated modestly, as GC1267 asks, so that it can be
falsified:* in this record and in the examples named here, every closed statement about one specific object came
from a finite certificate, a valuation or digit obstruction, an exact identity with a proved uniform error bound,
or an explicit construction of the object for the property; no finite measurement closed one. "Structure" means
those four things and nothing wider (Condrey's boundary, Proposition 19's cancellation, the sign rule's valuations,
the kick cone's certificates, Conway's atoms, Champernowne's construction). A proved statistical identity with a
uniform error bound counts as a theorem about a named sequence; the record's measurements do not. The claim is
falsified by one proof, for a sequence generated by a quiescent local rule from a finite seed that is neither
eventually periodic nor automatic, of any finite-state-observer property (simple normality upward) by means outside
those four.

**4. The selection rule, as a heuristic and not a theorem.** Razborov and Rudich's barrier is an analogy here
(GC1265), but the pattern in item 3 is a fact of the record. It gives a rule for choosing blocks, in GC1267's
