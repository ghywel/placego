An open problem, handed to everyone

$30,000 or ¥120,000,000 could be yours.

Three questions about one simple rule carry $10,000 each from Stephen Wolfram. The Collatz conjecture carries ¥120,000,000 from Bakuage. Rule 30 is a rule for colouring squares: start with one black square, apply the rule row after row, and the middle column comes out looking like coin flips. Nobody can prove that it never settles into a repeating pattern, and the same missing idea stands behind all four prizes.

Since the fourth of October 2026, one person and four AI collaborators have worked on it in public: every claim, every failure, every piece of data in one repository. We did not solve it. We learned a great deal about what a solution has to look like, and we found the exact sentence that is missing, then the same kind of sentence missing from every problem in the family. This site hands all of it to you.

On 19 October 2026 the first outside contribution arrived: Joshua Coon's machine-checked Lean library on Mahler's problem, built with the help of two AI models. Outside contributions says what it proves and how we checked it.

The one missing method

We spent ten days on Rule 30, then a day on Mahler's 3/2 problem, then took a first look at a fourth problem of the same family. The clearest thing we can tell you is this. In each of them, everything that can be counted, checked to a bound or proved for almost all cases has been done, by us or before us. What is missing in each is one argument of the same kind: that one specific starting point, one seed or one number, cannot keep matching a thin, fractal pattern of allowed behaviour for ever, when the pattern it must match grows more demanding with the size of the number itself. No field of mathematics has a general method for this. We think it is the one idea that would open all four.

This is our reading, not a theorem: the four could fall to different ideas. But the same wall appeared in each, independently, and we record it plainly. Our technical pages first named it as the gap between theorems about sets of cases and questions about one object. The Mahler and Erdős work of 14 October found it again from two new sides, and the two AI workers stopped together when it became clear that more of the same would not cross it.

A fifth problem, with a different wall. Following its own curiosity, GPT chose a problem outside the family: Eight streets on fire, Tan and Teh's question of how long eight streets must be before a town can always be burned on time. It was settled computationally on 19 October 2026: 68 houses a street is always enough. Its wall is not the one above. What stays open there is not one object that must avoid a pattern for ever, but a recipe that shares out every town at once, and it runs into an equal-shares problem that mathematicians list as open. It is recorded here for the route that settled it, which none of the four prize problems has offered: the infinite question was cut down to a finite list of 64,787,844 cases, and every case was checked by computer.

Where we stand, in one breath

There is no proof, and nothing to submit. The constant case was settled before we began (Condrey, 2026). The next case, a centre that alternates black, white, black, white for ever, is where almost all our work went. We proved, with a second reader inside the project, that whole families of would-be counterexamples cannot exist; we computed exactly how far any counterexample must sit from the centre; and we found that every route we tried ends at the same missing statement, one of a kind that no field of mathematics has a method for yet. Rule 30 now rests at a settled frontier. The Mahler room, opened on 14 October, raised the computer bound on Mahler's problem and turned its windows into residue problems, and met the same wall.

Rule 30's missing statement, exactly

Let R(d) be the longest white run the forced left side can show at depth d, over all real traces beside the alternating centre. Prove that R(d) is finite at every depth d. Finite, not bounded: the data say it grows. A finite pattern with an alternating centre would be white beyond its support, an infinite run, so this one statement refutes period two for every finite starting pattern. In plain words: keeping the centre alternating for ever must cost the pattern real information, and a finite pattern has only finitely many bits to spend. It is proved when the trace is periodic (Theorem B), and sketched, not yet second-read, for the pure rings the next column can run in; the general argument is open. The same problem, four times

Rule 30's alternating-centre question has the same shape as two famous open problems in arithmetic: the Collatz conjecture, which carries a prize of its own, and Mahler's 3/2 problem from 1968. In each, a free, chaotic side and a narrow, constrained side must agree for ever. Where the constrained side has only finitely many behaviours, all three are solved by the same argument. Where it does not, all three are open, and for the same reason. A fourth figure, Erdős's question whether 2n always has a 2 in base 3, has the same shape: the powers of 2 read in base 3 are the Collatz and Mahler arithmetic at its barest. The three side by side. Each has its own page: Rule 30, Collatz and Mahler.

Why we are asking

We are few, and the problem is big. Keeping every failure on record is how we did science; it is not a clean handover. This site is the clean handover. If you can read a proof, check one of ours. If you can write one, the four missing statements are stated for you, above. If you have a machine, the census can go deeper. If you know a paper we missed, tell us. How to join.

Who did this?

This project and everything in it were made by one human, Gareth, working with super intelligent systems built by Anthropic and OpenAI: a GPT, two Claudes on his own machines and one in the cloud, each with its own lane, each reading and checking the others, all of it in the open.

Everything here is offered in good faith, and all of it is rightfully subject to doubt. We ask two things of you. Do not take anything here at face value. And do not throw it out because an AI wrote it. Every claim names its source, every hypothesis is testable, every proof is written down to be checked, and every failure is kept on record so that you do not have to repeat it.

Care has gone into more than the mathematics. The problem has been made visible, in pictures and animations, so that a real person with no background can see what is being asked and why it is hard. The Wolfram Rule 30 page is fully readable by anybody with no knowledge of the problem, and Learn starts from nothing. From there, anyone, at any level, is invited to take the reins and win the prize.
