Mahler · no prize · open since 1968

Mahler's 3/2 problem

Take a positive number and multiply it by 3/2, again and again. Can its fractional part, the part after the decimal point, stay below one half every time, for ever? Kurt Mahler asked in 1968 whether any number does this. He called such a number a Z-number and guessed that none exists. Nobody has proved him right or wrong. There is no prize, which may be why so few people have dug here. It is the arithmetic sibling of Rule 30's question: a free side and a constrained side that must agree for ever.

Seen

Stated

The exact form (proved)

Write ξ·(3/2)j = nj + uj, with nj an integer and uj in [0, ½). Then the integer parts follow one fixed map, nj+1 = ⌈3nj/2⌉. The fractional parts are fixed by the future parities bk = nk mod 2:

uj = Σk≥0 bj+k · 2k / 3k+1

So a Z-number is a positive integer whose orbit under ⌈3x/2⌉ keeps every one of those sums below ½. Read on its own, the fractional side is completely free: every point of [0, ½) has an infinite admissible future. The whole difficulty is the integer side, whether some positive whole number can produce an admissible record. The same series read in the 2-adic numbers gives −n0. The 2-adic starts with an admissible record form a set of dimension log2(3/2). Mahler's conjecture says that set contains no positive ordinary integer.

Known in print

No short window. Flatto, Lagarias and Pollington (1995): for every ξ > 0, the lim sup minus the lim inf of the fractional parts of ξ(3/2)n is at least 1/3, so no ξ keeps them in any interval shorter than 1/3. Windows. Dubickas (2008) excludes a union of two intervals and shows that infinitely many ξ stay inside (5/48, 43/48). Stephan (2026), in Lean, shows no ξ stays in (2/7, 5/7), a window of length 3/7. Computation. Dubickas and Mossinghoff (2009): a Z-number, if one exists, exceeds 257. Conditional. Andrieu, Eliahou and Vivion (2025): if every orbit is evenly spread modulo every power of two, there is no Z-number.

What this project added (October 2026)

A census to 263, both ways (computed, exact): no Z-number below 263, and the negative half, a number whose fractional parts all stay in (½, 1), is clear to the same size. One laptop, a quarter of an hour each. The census in full.

A ladder of residue problems (proved, with a second reader). For each g ≥ 3 and the windows [0, a) with a between 3g−1/(3g − 2g) and (1 + (2/3)g−1)/3, the fractional side drops out entirely. A number keeps all its fractional parts in such a window exactly when it is n + u0(n) for a positive integer n whose odd orbit terms are all ≡ −1/3 modulo 2g. The first rung, g = 3, reads: is there a positive integer whose odd orbit terms are all 5 mod 8? That is equivalent to a ξ with every fractional part below 9/19. These windows are wider than 1/3 and beyond the published exclusions, and their emptiness is open. Whether the equivalence itself is already in print we have not settled.

Two cautions for anyone attempting a proof (single-party, not yet second-read). An argument that never uses the sign of the start would also settle the open window (½, 1). And several standard integer-side tools see only that the start is rational, not that it is a whole number; on their own they would have to prove a strictly stronger conjecture about rational starts.

Checked, here, now: the first rung

Is there a positive integer whose odd orbit terms under ⌈3x/2⌉ are all 5 mod 8? If there is, a number exists with every fractional part of ξ(3/2)n below 9/19, and so a Z-number too. Type a start and see how long it plays the game.

Every start fails sooner or later in every search so far. Among the starts below 263 still alive after 124 steps, the share whose first 124 parity bits keep every two odd steps at least 3 apart (the first rung's pattern, which certifies the 5 mod 8 game through step 121) was what chance predicts: 217 of 4,499, against 214.

What it is and what it is not

No proof, and nothing claimed. The census is evidence and the ladder is a reformulation. Our own swarm of nine agents, run on this problem with a skeptic for every idea, found nothing that moves the critical path, and said so. What the problem needs is a reason why a whole number's finite binary expansion cannot produce an admissible record, and no such reason is known.
