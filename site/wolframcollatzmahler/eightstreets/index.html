A puzzle about fire, planks and fair shares

Eight streets on fire

A fire spreads along a row of houses, and once a day you may light one new house anywhere. One street is easy. Eight separate streets, sharing one pile of fires, is an open question. This page walks from the first match to the wall we have reached, with every picture computed live.

1 · One street

Odd numbers, and a square

Watch the first fire. By the end of day one it has burned one house. By day two it has spread to three, by day three to five: a fire lit on day one has reached a stretch of 2m − 1 houses by day m. The fire you light on day two is a day behind, so it covers 2m − 3; the last one, lit on day m itself, has burned a single house. The stretches you command have the odd lengths 1, 3, 5, … up to 2m − 1, one of each.

Add the odd numbers and you get a square: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16, 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25. The picture below is the old reason why. So m days of fire burn at most m² houses, and a street of exactly m² houses is burned in m days by laying the stretches end to end, longest first, which is what the animation above does.

ExactOn a path of n vertices, a vertex burned at round k has burned a sub-path of at most 2(m − k) + 1 vertices by round m; the m sub-paths have odd sizes 1, 3, …, 2m − 1 and total at most m². The burning number of a graph is the least m for which some choice of sources burns everything by round m. For a single path of n vertices it is ⌈√n⌉ (Bonato, Janssen and Roshanbin, 2016, from the record's prior-art file).

2 · Eight streets

Planks for eight gaps

Now the town has eight separate streets, and the fire cannot jump from one to another. You still get one new fire a day, and the same odd stretches: after 28 days, one stretch of each length 1, 3, 5, …, 55. The only question is how to share them. Each street must receive stretches that add up to exactly its own length, because a stretch that overhangs a street's end is wasted, and a street with a gap left is not burned.

It helps to stop thinking about fire for a moment. You hold 28 planks of odd lengths 1, 3, 5, …, 55. There are eight gaps to tile. No plank may be cut, no plank may be left over, and every gap must be filled exactly. When the eight lengths add up to 28² = 784, the planks are exactly enough. Whether they can be arranged is the puzzle.

3 · When sharing fails

Four streets of 67, four of 102

Sharing can fail. Take 26 days, so the planks are 1, 3, …, 51, and let four streets be 67 houses long and four be 102. The lengths add up to 676 = 26², so the planks are exactly enough. Yet no arrangement works, and the reason fits in three lines.

A street of 102 cannot be tiled with two planks, because the two longest, 51 and 49, reach only 100. Any arrangement that avoids leaving a plank over uses whole planks, so each 102-street needs at least three; and since 102 is even and every plank is odd, it needs an even number, so at least four. A street of 67 needs an odd number of planks, and one plank is too short, so at least three. That is 4 × 4 + 4 × 3 = 28 planks, and there are only 26.

So a street can be too short to be safe. The 67-streets are the trouble: they can only take an odd number of planks, and the small planks they would need are wanted elsewhere. The natural question is how long every street must be before sharing always works, whatever the lengths.

4 · The question

Is 68 always enough?

Tan and Teh asked this for towns of k streets and settled it for one street up to seven. For seven streets the threshold is 56: if every street has at least 56 houses and the lengths add to a square m², then m days always suffice. For eight streets the example above shows 67 is not enough. Whether 68 always is, for every number of days and every eight lengths, is open. It is the problem GPT chose when the owner asked it to follow its own instincts, and the problem this page is about.

5 · Shrinking the pile

Only nine days' worth of cases matter

"Every number of days" sounds infinite, and it is, but a trick tames it. If some street is very long, give it the longest plank, 2m − 1, and put it aside: what remains is the same puzzle one day shorter. Run that backwards and any case with a street of at least 2m + 67 houses follows from a smaller case. The cases that do not follow from anything smaller have every street between 68 and 2m + 66, and once m reaches 33 there are none at all, because eight such streets cannot add up to 33².

So the whole infinite question comes down to the days 24 to 32. That is still a pile: 64,787,844 cases, most of them at 27 and 28 days. Checking them one by one is a large computation; the record has measured it, and it is the subject of the last section.

ExactStrong induction on m: if some target li ≥ 2m + 67, remove the plank 2m − 1 from it; the reduced instance has total (m − 1)², every target still at least 68, and is solved by the inductive hypothesis. The exceptional set Em (all eight targets in [68, 2m + 66], total m²) is empty for m ≥ 33, so L8 ≤ 68 follows from direct certificates on E24 … E32. Proposed by Cloud, audited by GPT (MI044).

6 · Taking the 1 off every plank

The same puzzle on 0, 1, 2, 3, …

Every plank has odd length, so every plank is "one house plus an even number". Saw one house off each. A plank of length 2i + 1 becomes a plank of length 2i, and halving the scale makes it i. The planks 1, 3, 5, …, 55 become 0, 1, 2, …, 27, the ordinary counting numbers. A street that wanted c planks adding to l now wants c numbers from 0 to 27 adding to (l − c)/2.

This is the form everything below uses. The pool is a run of consecutive numbers; each street says how many it will take and what they must add to. One rule is obviously necessary: a street taking c numbers cannot ask for less than the c smallest add to, nor more than the c largest. And the same for any group of streets together: two streets taking seven numbers between them cannot ask for less than 0 + 1 + … + 6. Call this the cut rule.

ExactWith L(c) = c(c − 1)/2 and a pool {0, …, n − 1}, a group J of bins with r numbers between them and total SJ must satisfy L(r) ≤ SJ ≤ r(n − 1) − L(r). Condition SS: this holds for every group. It is necessary. Whether it is sufficient is the question of the next section.

7 · What has been proved

On a plain run of numbers, the cut rule is the whole story

Here is the surprise that made the problem tractable. For three streets, and for four, the cut rule is not just necessary but sufficient: if every group of streets sits inside its band, an exact sharing exists. The four-street case was conjectured from a complete check of about 250,000 small instances with no exception, and then proved by hand, with the proof read line by line by a second party. The proof is constructive: it finds the tightest street or pair of streets, gives it a specific staircase-shaped set of numbers, and hands the rest to the three-street theorem.

For the real problem the streets' lengths are not arbitrary, and here the record holds something stronger than a theorem: a certificate. For every one of the 258,180 cases at 28 days in which all eight streets have odd length, an explicit sharing was computed, written down, and independently recomputed and checked line by line by a second program. Those cases are done.

8 · The hole

Why eight streets are not four streets twice

The natural plan writes itself. Take the tightest street, give it its numbers by the recipe, remove them, and you are left with a smaller puzzle: seven streets on what remains. Repeat until the four-street theorem finishes it. The trouble is the second step. What remains is no longer a plain run of numbers. The removed street has punched holes in it.

And with a hole, the cut rule stops being enough. Here is the smallest failure. The pool is 0 to 12 with 6 missing; four streets take three numbers each, with totals 4, 18, 18 and 32. Every group sits inside its band. But the street wanting 4 has only one way to get it, {0, 1, 3}, and the street wanting 32 only one, {9, 11, 12}; and what is left cannot make 18 twice. A pinned street spoils everything.

The repair is a margin. Ask each group of streets to clear its lower band not just by zero but by one unit per number it takes, as if the hole had been pushed to the bottom of the pool. With that margin the pinned sets disappear, and the one-hole, four-street statement holds in every case checked, nearly half a million of them. Two holes need twice the margin, and then it holds again. GPT proved the exact form of the margin the real descent produces (MI362), and proved that it is inherited when the tightest street is removed, whichever of its possible sets it takes (MI368).

ExactP0: on {0, …, m} \ {q}, four sizes ≥ 3, correct total, and SJ ≥ L(r) + r for every proper group J, a partition exists. Machine-true for m ≤ 15 (475,931 instances, CL440); hand proof open. With two holes and margin 2r: true for m ≤ 13; with margin r: false.

9 · The wall

Three holes, equal shares, and a known hard case

Then the descent reaches three holes, and the margin stops being enough. The failures are rare, one in a hundred thousand, and they all look the same: the streets want equal totals, and the pool has split into two clumps. Here is the smallest. The pool is 0, 1, 2, 3 and 7, 8, 9, 10, 11; three streets take three numbers each and each wants 17. A street holding two of the small numbers can reach at most 2 + 3 + 11 = 16, so each street holds at most one small number. But there are four small numbers and only three streets.

Counting the clumps repairs some of these but not all; a few survive, always with equal totals and a tight band. That is not an accident. Sharing a run of numbers into equal sums, with every share at least three numbers, is a question the literature lists as open (Hoory and Kotlar, 2026). The descent from eight streets, pushed far enough, lands on a thin disguise of that known open problem. This is where the theorem road stands tonight: everything up to two holes is in hand, and the hard core beyond is recognisable.

ExactMargin family T(h), three bins of sizes ≥ 3 and lower margin h·r: zero failures for h ≤ 2 (269,874 instances, M ≤ 14); failures 1, 3, 5 at h = 3, 4, 5, all with equal targets. Adding the necessary count-capacity condition at every cut point leaves 0, 2, 4. (CL443, 18 October 2026.) 10 · Where we stand, and what we need

Two roads to 68

The theorem road. Write the theorem that is actually true: the cut rule with margins and clump counting suffices up to two holes, with an explicitly named family of exceptions beyond. Then show that the real descent from eight streets never lands in the exceptional family. The pieces proved so far are the rungs; the missing rung is a rule for choosing which set the tightest street takes so that the next level stays inside the family. Half the time any choice works; the other half needs the mirror-image family, and whether the two close on each other is one bounded test away.

The certificate road. The reduction of section 5 says 64.8 million explicit sharings would settle L8 ≤ 68 outright, the way the 258,180 all-odd cases are already settled. A plain search takes about 143 milliseconds per case; that is months on one core and days with a better solver on a modern laptop. It is a computation the record has measured and not yet run.

What would help

A faster exact solver for "c numbers from a punctured run with a prescribed sum, eight times over"; eyes on the equal-sum core with holes; and, for the visual reader, pictures. Every obstruction on this page was found by machine and only afterwards seen: the pinned set, the clumps, the pigeonholes. If a picture makes you see the next one before the machine does, that is a contribution, and here is how to send it.
